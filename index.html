Behavioural science case study

One barcode. Two shoppers. Opposite verdicts.

Yuka scores every product the same way for every shopper. This case study diagnoses why that isn't enough, using a real product whose correct verdict depends entirely on who's asking — and designs the fix around the mechanism, not the symptom.

Read the case study ↓

1. The diagnostic hook

By its own published method [S5] verified, Yuka scores a product on three fixed weights: 60% nutritional quality (calculated on a Nutri-Score basis), 30% additives — where a single high-risk additive caps the product at 49/100 — and a 10% organic bonus. It is a well-built tool: one science-based nutrition-and-additives signal, delivered instantly, to a wide audience.

It is also, by construction, identical for every user. The published method takes no input for an individual's dietary constraint — a diagnosed condition, an intolerance, a personal goal. That is not a failing; it is a design fact with one structural consequence, and it is the starting point for everything below. Food-Checker is not "better than Yuka." It targets a different step: Yuka evaluates the product, Food-Checker removes the personal-translation step that comes after.

2. Behavioural diagnosis

[S6] verified COM-B explains what a generic score does and doesn't fix. It gives the shopper Capability — an evaluation they couldn't compute themselves — and Opportunity — instant, in-store. It does not resolve Motivation-in-context: acting on a personal goal the score itself doesn't represent.

Dual-process theory [S7] verified [S8] verified names why that gap persists. Reading a label against a personal constraint — "does this trigger my IBS, at this portion?" — is slow, deliberative reasoning. The shopper is doing it under fast, low-attention shopping conditions. A generic score doesn't remove that step; personalising the verdict does.

Cognitive load [S9] inferred explains why the cost compounds. Holding a trigger list, scanning an ingredient list, and judging a dose threshold, all at the shelf, is real working-memory load — and it rises with every constraint stacked on top. This is the mechanism behind the interview-reported cost of "spending ages in store" (see Evidence, below).

3. Intervention design

Two features map directly onto the two points of failure above, one for one — not a longer feature list, a targeted one.

A personalised verdict removes the translation step entirely. The shopper's constraint is the input to the evaluation, not a mental step they have to perform afterwards on a generic score.

A plain-language "why" behind every verdict addresses a need named independently by all four interview participants [S13] verified: not just a flag, but the reason for it. A flag with no reason just adds a new judgement call — it doesn't remove one.

That direction is inferred from Yuka's published method, not from a scan of this specific product — it is not presented here as a measured score.

The interview evidence

Four individual, anonymised interviews [S13] verified — not a survey, not a focus group — each describe the same friction from a different starting point: a self-observed skin-related avoidance pattern (P01, explicitly her own perceived pattern, not a clinical finding), a dietitian-derived IBS trigger list (P02), self-composed longevity principles (P03), and clinical pre-diabetes guidance (P04). All four independently describe label-reading that is slow and easy to get wrong, made harder by unfamiliar ingredient names, competing criteria, or dose-dependent thresholds.

These four interviews are formative, not representative. They illustrate that the same mechanism recurs across different starting points; they are not evidence of prevalence, market size, or clinical effectiveness of any diet. Full methodology in the Evidence & Methodology appendix.

5. Scope & limitations

Sustainability and environmental-impact scoring [EX1] excluded is out of scope here. This is a data-reliability scoping cut, not a claim that sustainability falls outside the behavioural mechanism — sustainability trade-offs plausibly happen under the same at-the-shelf, high-load conditions as health trade-offs. It's excluded because the underlying data (food miles, lifecycle assessment, packaging) is too unreliable to build a credible intervention on, and mixing a data-reliability problem into a behavioural-friction argument would muddy both.

This case study makes no medical, clinical-effectiveness, prevalence, market-size, or willingness-to-pay claims. It is about decision friction at the point of purchase, not dietary advice.

Interview methodology

Four individual, one-to-one interviews, each covering one person's food-shopping behaviour around one personal dietary constraint or goal. They are formative, not representative: they show that a friction recurs across different starting points, not how common it is, how many people would want a fix, or whether any diet is clinically effective. No participant is quoted directly — consent to quote was not recorded in the source transcripts — so everything above is paraphrase. Each participant is anonymised as P01–P04.

About this project

This is a personal portfolio case study in applied behavioural science: a worked example of diagnosing a real, sourced gap in a market-leading product and designing an intervention that targets the mechanism, not the symptom. It is not a funded venture, and it is not a claim that Food-Checker is superior to any incumbent product.
