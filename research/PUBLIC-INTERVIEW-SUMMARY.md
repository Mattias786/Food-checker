## Status of this evidence

- **Four individual, one-to-one interviews.** Not a survey, not a focus group, not a
  larger informal sample. Each interview covered one person's food-shopping behaviour
  in relation to one personal dietary constraint or goal.
- **Formative, not representative.** These four interviews illustrate that a particular
  decision-friction mechanism recurs across different starting points. They are not a
  representative sample of any population and cannot be used to argue that this
  friction is common, rare, or typical.
- **No direct quotations appear in this summary.** The transcripts supplied do not
  record explicit participant consent to be quoted directly in a public case study.
  Everything below is paraphrase. See "Consent status" below before adding any direct
  quotation to a published version.
- **Recruitment method not recorded.** How these four people were reached is not
  stated in the transcripts provided. Confirm and document this before describing
  recruitment publicly (e.g. do not assert "informal conversations within a personal
  network" unless that is actually confirmed).

## What this evidence can and cannot support

**Can support:**
- That the same underlying friction — translating a personal dietary goal into an
  in-the-moment product decision — recurs across four different starting points: a
  self-observed skin-related pattern, a dietitian-derived trigger list, a set of
  self-composed long-term health principles, and clinical guidance following a
  pre-diabetes result.
- The eight recurring themes documented below, each grounded in specific statements
  from at least one interview, and in most cases all four.
- Descriptive detail about what these four people said would make the decision
  easier (fast, explained, non-absolute, personalisable).

**Cannot support — do not present as if they do:**
- **Prevalence.** How common this friction is in the wider population.
- **Market size.** How many people would want or use a product like this.
- **Clinical effectiveness.** Whether any dietary approach described here is medically
  effective. In particular, one participant's account of a link between her diet and
  her skin is **her own perceived pattern, stated by her as such** — she explicitly
  says she does not know the underlying science and is going by what she has noticed.
  This must not be presented as an established or clinically verified diet-acne
  relationship anywhere in the case study.
- **Willingness to pay.** None of the four discussed price, payment, or purchase
  intent for any product.

## Recurring themes across the four interviews

1. **Difficulty translating a broad dietary goal into a specific in-store product
   decision.** Present in all four (P01–P04).
2. **Label and ingredient complexity** — unfamiliar ingredient names, and on-pack
   claims that don't match the ingredient list. Present in all four.
3. **Time pressure and social pressure while shopping** — queues, people waiting,
   children present, unfamiliar/crowded stores. Present in all four.
4. **Falling back on familiar or basic products** when uncertain or rushed, rather
   than trying something that might better fit the goal. Present in all four.
5. **Uncertainty caused by competing criteria** — a product can look favourable on one
   measure and unfavourable on another, with no clear way to weigh them. Most explicit
   in P03 and P04; also present in P01 and P02.
6. **Desire for a fast, personalised explanation** rather than a generic verdict.
   Present in all four.
7. **Desire to understand why a product was flagged**, not just to receive a score or
   label. Present in all four.
8. **Distrust of absolute or medical-sounding claims**, and a preference for cautious,
   non-alarming language that doesn't imply every choice is a failure. Present in all
   four.
